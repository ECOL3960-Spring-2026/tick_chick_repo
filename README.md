# An Investigation into Abundance of Tick-borne Pathogens
#### Authors: Claire Morgan, Lindsey Stowell, Madison McDonald, Natalie Meyers



## Description of Project

During this project, our team will examine how mean seasonal temperatures affect the abundance of ticks infected by pathogens in the Northeast and Southeast. We have chosen this question because we are looking to explore the effects of climate change on human health and wildlife wellbeing. We chose the Northeast due to the disproportionate prevalence of tick-borne diseases in the human population compared to other regions of the United States, and we chose the Southeast due to the ideal year-round climate supporting tick activity and abundance, as well as to compare regional differences in pathogen types across different regions.

Our main hypothesis is that as temperature increases, the number of ticks present in the area will increase and the proportion of ticks that are infected by a pathogen increase. Our null hypothesis is that variations in temperature have no effect on the number of ticks present, nor the proportion of ticks that are infected by a pathogen.

Our alternative hypothesis is that as as temperature increases, the abundance of ticks and the number of ticks that are infected by a pathogen will increase, until a certain temperature barrier/threshold, where they then decrease, creating a parabolic shape in tick distribution.

To test this, we will compare data on mean seasonal temperatures at the HARV site to the proportion of nymphal ticks that were sampled and were positive for pathogens at the HARV site -- we will recreate this process with the SCBI, SERC, TREE, UKFS, and BLAN sites. 

## Planned Data to Use

We plan to use these NEON datasets:

-   DP1.10092.001 - Tick pathogen status (testResult, maybe testPathogenName) This dataset will inform whether the ticks are infected by pathogens or not as well as the abundance of nymphal ticks sampled. -

-   DP1.00098.001 - Relative humidity (tempRHMean)


The data includes the measures for tick pathogen status, relative humidity, and nymphal ticks sampled from drag cloths. For our analysis, we are using data in sites HARV, SCBI, SERC, TREE, UKFS, and BLAN due to the proportion of infected nymphal ticks being relatively large (above 0.5, or 50%) of data points in these sites. We are retrieving our data from NEON database to support or reject our hypothesis with existing data.
